\begin{abstract}
\noindent
The 2026 BEHAVIOR Challenge asks one system to complete 100 long-horizon household tasks in simulation. Rather than train a single generalist, we assemble a bank of ten vision-language-action checkpoints, nine public and one continued by us, and route each task to one of them. Routing uses development instances only: an empirical-Bayes model combines public reference scores, policy levels estimated against a reference policy, and our eligible development rollouts (\DevEligible{} in total, \DevAtFreeze{} available when the main route froze), and conservative switching rules guard each change of policy. The frozen route reaches $\Q=\Qval$ (95\% CI \Qlo--\Qhi) on the ten public reporting instances of every task; \Nnonzero{} tasks score above zero and \Nsuccess{} of \Nepisodes{} episodes, in \Ntaskssuccess{} tasks, succeed. Development estimates ranked tasks moderately well (Spearman \Spearman) but were optimistic by \OptimismGap{} on average, and the gap sits in tasks whose estimate rested on a prior or a single rollout. A posterior-predictive simulation of our own procedure finds that, within the model, ranking by raw development means delivers a worse route and claims about 0.12 more than it delivers, while perfect knowledge of the candidates would add only about 0.04 on the new tasks. We release the route, all \Nepisodes{} metric files, the development ledger, the selector, the simulator and a fail-stop serving layer.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

BEHAVIOR-1K~\cite{li2024behavior1k,li2023behavior1k} defines everyday household activities in realistic simulation, and its 2026 challenge~\cite{behavior2026challenge} scores a single submission on 100 of them. The tasks are long: mean human demonstrations run from about one to fifteen minutes, and an episode ends when the goal is reached or when the robot has used 1.5 times the mean demonstration length. Partial credit counts the fraction of goal conditions satisfied, and the challenge score \Q{} averages it over tasks.

By 2026 several strong public checkpoints existed, all from the $\pi_0$/$\pi_{0.5}$ vision-language-action family~\cite{black2024pi0,pi05,openpi}: the Comet team's 2025 models~\cite{bai2025comet}, the four checkpoints of the 2025 winning solution~\cite{larchenko2025task}, continuations of these on the 2026 demonstrations, and community specialists fine-tuned for a handful of tasks. Their quality differs sharply by task. With a fixed compute budget, we ask a practical question: \emph{which checkpoint should run each task, decided from development data alone, without fooling ourselves with noise?}

This is per-instance algorithm selection~\cite{rice1976algorithm,kerschke2019survey} with three complications. Evidence is scarce: a rollout runs for up to 22 simulated minutes and longer in wall-clock time, so most (task, policy) pairs get one to three development rollouts. Rollouts are noisy: the same policy on the same task scores 0 on one instance and 1 on the next. And picking the best-looking option from noisy estimates is optimistically biased~\cite{smith2006optimizer,efron2011tweedie}. We therefore treat routing as shrinkage estimation~\cite{efron1975stein}: every (task, policy) pair gets an informative prior, development rollouts update it, and conservative rules decide when a challenger may replace the incumbent.

The frozen route scored $\Q=\Qval$ on the public reporting instances. More useful than the score are three findings about selection at this scale. First, the development estimates were optimistic, but the optimism sits where evidence was thinnest, not where selection was most aggressive. Second, in a posterior-predictive simulation of our own procedure, ranking by raw development means picks a worse route and overclaims by about 0.12, while the posterior picks a better route and claims it honestly. Third, within the model the candidate pool, not the selector, limits the score: an oracle choosing among the same candidates gains only about 0.04 on the new tasks.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{enumerate}
\item A \emph{policy bank} of ten checkpoints, each served behind its native adapter, and a frozen per-task route chosen without looking at reported instances (Sections~\ref{sec:bank}--\ref{sec:routing}).
\item A complete specification of the selector: priors with and without public reference results, conjugate updates, switching rules and hyperparameters (Algorithm~\ref{alg:route}, Table~\ref{tab:hyper}).
\item An audited evaluation: $\Q=\Qval$ [\Qlo, \Qhi] on \Nepisodes{} unchanged episodes, with score-blind duplicate handling and every deviation disclosed (Sections~\ref{sec:protocol}--\ref{sec:results}).
\item An analysis of calibration, a posterior-predictive simulation of the selection rules, and the statistical power of development comparisons (Section~\ref{sec:analysis}).
\item A fail-stop serving layer for hidden evaluation, with an adapter for OpenPI-compatible policy servers, and an open release of code, data and route (Section~\ref{sec:serving}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related work}

\paragraph{Vision-language-action policies.} $\pi_0$~\cite{black2024pi0} attaches a flow-matching action expert to a PaliGemma vision-language backbone~\cite{beyer2024paligemma}, and $\pi_{0.5}$~\cite{pi05} adds co-training for open-world generalization. Every checkpoint in our bank descends from this line through OpenPI~\cite{openpi}.

\paragraph{BEHAVIOR solutions.} The 2025 winning solution~\cite{larchenko2025task} adapted $\pi_{0.5}$ with correlated-noise flow matching, stage tracking and task-specific correction rules, reaching \Q{}$\approx$0.26 on 50 tasks. The Comet team's solution~\cite{bai2025comet}, second in 2025, studied pre-training and post-training recipes at scale. Apart from one continued-training run, we train nothing; our contribution is to select among such models and to measure the result carefully.

\paragraph{Selection under noise.} Choosing the best of several noisy alternatives is the classical ranking-and-selection problem~\cite{bechhofer1954} and, sequentially, best-arm identification~\cite{kaufmann2016bestarm}. Empirical Bayes and Stein shrinkage~\cite{efron1975stein} improve many noisy means at once by borrowing strength across them, and Tweedie's formula corrects the selection bias of reported winners~\cite{efron2011tweedie}. Unadjusted estimates of selected options disappoint on average (the optimizer's curse~\cite{smith2006optimizer}), and underpowered comparisons exaggerate effect sizes (Type~M errors~\cite{gelman2014typem}). In learning research, reliable evaluation with few runs per task has been argued for in reinforcement learning~\cite{agarwal2021precipice}. We apply these ideas to routing robot policies under a fixed evaluation budget.

\section{Problem setup}
\label{sec:setup}

\paragraph{Tasks and scoring.} Each of the 100 tasks $t$ has a goal written in BDDL. An episode ends at success or when the simulator step count reaches 1.5 times the mean demonstration length for $t$. Its score $q\in[0,1]$ is the fraction of goal conditions satisfied at the end, with $q=1$ exactly at success. With $I=\{301,\dots,310\}$ the reporting instances,
\begin{equation}
\Q=\frac{1}{100}\sum_{t=1}^{100}\frac{1}{|I|}\sum_{i\in I} q_{t,i},
\end{equation}
where a missing cell counts as zero. Instances 311--320 are reserved for development (our ledger contains rollouts on 311--319); the organizers evaluate submitted containers on hidden instances 321--340.

\paragraph{Robot and interface.} The robot is a wheeled bimanual R1Pro. A policy receives head and wrist RGB-D images, a 61-dimensional proprioceptive vector and the task identity, and returns 23-dimensional actions over a websocket, optionally several per query. Hidden evaluation requires an average of at least one simulator frame per second including inference, at most 600\,s per query, and a single 24\,GB GPU per policy\pl.

\paragraph{Rules we imposed on ourselves.} Policies use only onboard sensors at test time. Routing decisions use development instances only. A route is frozen and hashed before the reported runs of the affected tasks, and each reported cell is scored from its first completed run.

\section{The policy bank}
\label{sec:bank}

Table~\ref{tab:bank} lists the ten routed checkpoints with their exact sources. They fall into three families.
\textbf{Comet} ($\pi_{0.5}$, replaying 32 actions per query): the released \code{pt50} and \code{pt12} checkpoints~\cite{bai2025comet,comethf}; a public continuation of \code{pt50} on the 2026 demonstrations by ymetz~\cite{ymetzhf} (step 9999); and \code{s150997}, our own continued training of \code{pt50} on the 2026 demonstrations of all 100 tasks.
\textbf{Winner} (replaying 20 actions per query): the four checkpoints of the 2025 winning solution~\cite{larchenko2025task,winnerhf}, served with their native wrapper and correction rules.
\textbf{Winner-format specialists}: two community models fine-tuned for a few 2026 tasks~\cite{jackhf,amyhf}.

Every checkpoint runs behind its own native adapter: prompts, image preprocessing, normalization statistics, action representation and replay length are those of its publisher. The bank is a lookup table from task to checkpoint; we do not merge weights or ensemble actions.

Our development ledger holds \DevRollouts{} rollouts over \DevLabels{} labels, including other checkpoint steps, a third-party 100-task model, action-search variants, time compression, gripper-correction add-ons and a modified robot configuration. A rollout is \emph{eligible} for selection when it ran on a development instance with the default configuration, no evaluation-time variant and a routable policy label: \DevEligible{} rollouts over \DevEligibleLabels{} labels qualify.

\section{Development-selected routing}
\label{sec:routing}

\subsection{Model}
\label{sec:model}
Let $\theta_{tp}$ be the expected score of policy $p$ on task $t$. We place a Gaussian prior $\theta_{tp}\sim\mathcal N(\mu_{tp},\tau_{tp}^2)$ and treat each development rollout as $y_{tpk}\sim\mathcal N(\theta_{tp},\sigma_p^2)$. With $n$ rollouts of mean $\bar y$, the posterior is
\begin{equation}
\theta_{tp}\mid y \sim \mathcal N\!\left(\frac{\mu_{tp}/\tau_{tp}^2 + n\bar y/\sigma_p^2}{1/\tau_{tp}^2+n/\sigma_p^2},\;\Big(\frac{1}{\tau_{tp}^2}+\frac{n}{\sigma_p^2}\Big)^{-1}\right).
\label{eq:post}
\end{equation}
The rollout variance is the pooled within-task variance of $p$'s development rollouts, shrunk toward a default: $\hat\sigma_p^2=\max\{0.01,\,(\mathrm{SS}_p+\nu_0\sigma_0^2)/(\mathrm{df}_p+\nu_0)\}$ with $\sigma_0^2=0.08$ and $\nu_0=4$. Rollout scores are bounded and often bimodal, so the Gaussian likelihood is an approximation whose job is to weigh a prior against a handful of rollouts. Section~\ref{sec:sim} checks it against zero-inflated Beta outcomes.

\subsection{Priors where public reference results exist (tasks 0--49)}
For the first 50 tasks, per-task results of the Comet and winner checkpoints from 2025 are public. They come from a different simulator setup and different instances, so we scale them by a transfer factor:
\begin{equation}
\mu_{tp}=\gamma_f\, r_{tp},\qquad \tau_{tp}^2=(r_{tp}\,s_{\gamma})^2+b^2,
\end{equation}
where $r_{tp}$ is the reference score, $s_\gamma$ the factor's standard error and $b=0.088$ a floor for task-specific deviations. For \code{pt50}, $\gamma$ is the ratio of our development means to the reference over the other tasks (leave-one-task-out), giving $0.55\pm0.09$\pl. For the winner family, $\gamma$ was fixed at $0.81\pm0.10$\pl{} before stage 2 and used for its decisions; an empirical-Bayes estimate from an early subset of winner rollouts gave $0.69\pm0.11$\pl. \code{pt12}, which has no published per-task results of its own, borrows the Comet reference scores\pl.

\subsection{Priors without reference results (tasks 50--99)}
Tasks 50--99 are new in 2026, so no per-task results exist. We estimate a \emph{policy level} instead. The reference policy \code{s150997} has eligible development rollouts on every task, so its level $\ell_{\mathrm{ref},-t}$ is the mean of its per-task means over the tasks other than $t$. A candidate's level is the reference level plus its mean paired difference on the other tasks both have run:
\begin{equation}
\ell_{p,-t}=\ell_{\mathrm{ref},-t}+\frac{1}{|S_{p,-t}|}\sum_{u\in S_{p,-t}}\big(\bar y_{up}-\bar y_{u,\mathrm{ref}}\big),\qquad S_{p,-t}=\{u\neq t:\ \text{both ran }u\}.
\end{equation}
Pairing removes much of the between-task variation. The level's standard error combines the reference level's error with the standard error of the mean paired difference, whose standard deviation is floored at 0.05 (and set to 0.25 when only one task is shared), treating the two as independent. Levels are clipped at 0. Each level is combined with a weak hyperprior $\mathcal N(0.1,0.1^2)$ and widened by a between-task standard deviation $\tau_b=0.1$ to give $(\mu_{tp},\tau_{tp})$.

\paragraph{Worked example.} On \code{vacuuming\_floors} (task 69), the released code gives the ymetz checkpoint a level of $\ExLevel\pm\ExLevelSd$, a prior of $\ExPrior\pm\ExPriorSd$ and, with rollout variance $\ExNoise$, a posterior of $\ExPost\pm\ExPostSd$ after its single development rollout scored 0. The original pipeline also pooled each level with the other step of the same training run: its candidate report of 12 October gave $0.184\pm0.111$ moving to $0.160$\pl{} for the same rollout, and the frozen table of 13 October, built from more rollouts, records 0.166.

\subsection{Switching rules}
A posterior ranking alone would chase noise. On the 13 tasks from 0--49 that were routed in stage~2 (Section~\ref{sec:freeze}), the incumbent came from an earlier route and a challenger must pass every rule:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Own evidence}: at least two of its own development rollouts on the task.
\item \textbf{Development guard}: it beats the incumbent on the development instances both ran, with at least two shared instances.
\item \textbf{Margin}: a posterior gain of at least 0.01 over the incumbent.
\item \textbf{Veto}: it is rejected if it scored 0 on every development instance where the incumbent scored above 0.1.
\end{enumerate}
On tasks 50--99 the incumbent is the weak reference policy, so the highest eligible posterior wins if it clears the \textbf{margin}, subject to the \textbf{veto} and two further rules:
\begin{enumerate}[resume]
\item \textbf{Scope}: a specialist is a candidate only on tasks where it has its own development rollouts, and only with at least two rollouts in total.
\item \textbf{One checkpoint per training run}: when two steps of the same training run (here ymetz steps 6000 and 9999) win different tasks, every step is tried as the keeper, including one that won nowhere; the others are removed, all tasks are re-decided, and the keeper whose route has the highest total expected score stays. This keeps the number of serving images small; the reference is never removed.
\end{enumerate}
The 37 stage-1 tasks had no incumbent. Their entries were written by an earlier version of the route writer, which ranked posteriors built from the same priors without the guard rules\pl.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Level-based routing for tasks without reference results (\code{select\_by\_level})}
\label{alg:route}
\small
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\Require eligible development rollouts $\mathcal D$, reference $r$, generalists $G$, specialists $S$, training-run map $F$
\State $\hat\sigma^2_p \gets$ pooled, shrunk rollout variance of every $p$ \Comment{Sec.~\ref{sec:model}}
\Function{Decide}{$t$, allowed}
  \State $C \gets (G\cup\{s\in S: s \text{ ran } t,\ |\mathcal D_s|\ge 2\})\cap$ allowed
  \For{$p\in C$}
     \State $\ell \gets \ell_{r,-t}$ if $p=r$ else $\ell_{p,-t}$ \Comment{leave task $t$ out}
     \State prior $\gets \mathrm{widen}(\mathrm{combine}(\ell,\mathcal N(0.1,0.1^2)),\tau_b)$
     \State post$_p \gets$ Eq.~\eqref{eq:post} with $p$'s rollouts on $t$
  \EndFor
  \State drop every $p$ vetoed on $t$
  \State $p^\star \gets \arg\max_{p\neq r}$ post$_p$
  \State \Return $p^\star$ if post$_{p^\star}-$post$_r\ge 0.01$ else $r$
\EndFunction
\State allowed $\gets G\cup S$;\quad route $\gets$ \Call{Decide}{$t$, allowed} for all $t$
\While{some training run $f$ has $\ge2$ members in route}
  \For{each member $k$ of $f$} \Comment{try every keeper}
     \State trial$_k \gets$ allowed $\setminus(f\setminus\{k\})$;\quad value$_k \gets\sum_t$ post of \Call{Decide}{$t$, trial$_k$}
  \EndFor
  \State allowed $\gets$ trial$_{\arg\max_k \text{value}_k}$;\quad route $\gets$ \Call{Decide}{$t$, allowed} for all $t$
\EndWhile
\State \Return route
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

Evaluation compute was the binding constraint, so routing was frozen in two stages (Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}). Stage~1 fixed \StageOneTasks{} tasks from 0--49 whose choice was already settled by priors and early development evidence; their reported runs began on 11 October. For \StageOneZeroOwn{} of them the routed policy had no own development rollouts on the task, so its expectation rested on its prior alone. \section{Evaluation protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}

\paragraph{Infrastructure.} Reported and development runs used TACC Stampede3 nodes with two NVIDIA RTX PRO 6000 Blackwell GPUs, running Isaac Sim 6.0.1 inside the organizers' container image. Isaac Sim 5.1, the version prescribed for hidden evaluation, crashed with the cluster's GPU driver; the organizers allowed Isaac Sim 6 for public results. Each simulator connects to its own policy server, because one native server keeps a single action plan for all its connections. Camera inputs use the full-resolution RGB-D wrapper: a paired development comparison over 38\pl{} cells found 224-pixel inputs worse by 0.061\pl{} on average. Over the project we used roughly 1,500\pl{} documented allocated GPU-hours, including idle and failed allocations.

\paragraph{Integrity rules.} The reporting set contains exactly one unchanged metrics file per (task, instance), 1,000 in total. All are released byte for byte in \code{data/metrics} with their SHA-256, and \code{policybank verify} re-checks any folder of evaluator files against them. Infrastructure retries produced two completed duplicate cells. In both we kept the attempt that \emph{finished first}, regardless of its score: \code{spraying\_for\_bugs}/307 (kept 0.5, later 0.0) and \code{cook\_brussels\_sprouts}/303 (kept 0.0, later 0.0). The run logs\pl{} show that each later attempt started after the kept one ended. No development rerun or later experiment was merged into the reporting set.

\paragraph{Execution details.} In the reporting set, \ReplayThirtyTwo{} episodes ran with replay 32, \ReplayTwenty{} with replay 20, and \ReplayZero{} came from the farm script's recovery pass, which replays no actions (one model query per control step). We report those seven unchanged. The copy of the organizers' scorer bundled with the simulator divides by a 20-instance public pool and returns 0.078 on our files; the published reporting contract uses ten instances, and the same unmodified functions with that denominator give \Qfull. We changed neither the scorer nor the files.

\subsection{Calibration}
\label{sec:calibration}
Figure~\ref{fig:calibration} compares each task's posterior expectation at freeze with its realized score. Across tasks the ranking holds moderately well (Spearman \Spearman, Pearson \Pearson). Because each point is a different task, this mostly measures how well task difficulty was predicted; whether the right policy was chosen within a task needs the simulation of Section~\ref{sec:sim}. Expectations were optimistic: the mean expectation was \MeanExpected{} and the mean realization \MeanRealized.

Table~\ref{tab:depth} shows where the gap sits. Grouped by how many own development rollouts the routed policy had on the task when its route froze, the expectation overshot by \DepthZeroAbs{} task points on the \DepthZeroTasks{} tasks whose expectation rested on the prior alone and by \DepthOneAbs{} on the \DepthOneTasks{} tasks with one rollout, but undershot by \DepthTwoAbs{} on the \DepthTwoTasks{} tasks with two or more. Split by stage, the stage-1 tasks, whose priors are transfer priors, overshot by 2.7 task points and the new tasks 50--99, whose priors are policy levels, by 2.6, while the 13 stage-2 tasks from 0--49 were slightly pessimistic. Two policies illustrate the pattern. At freeze, the ymetz checkpoint's development mean on its 42 routed tasks (0.171) was no higher than on all 50 new tasks (0.176), so it was not chosen on lucky rollouts; but at freeze 39 of its 50 tasks had a single eligible rollout and 68\% of its eligible rollouts used instance 313, and its tasks account for 3.2 of the 5.0 gap points. Conversely, the four-task specialist's raw development mean (0.64) was shrunk to an expectation of 0.37 and it delivered 0.58. The optimism therefore points at expectations built on thin, concentrated evidence, under both prior types, rather than at aggressive selection; the simulation below quantifies the selection part.

\subsection{Simulating the selection procedure}
\label{sec:sim}
The route can be scored only once, so we ask counterfactual questions with a model fitted to our own data (\code{policybank.simulate}). Each of \Sims{} simulated worlds draws a true score $\theta_{tp}$ for every (task, candidate) cell on tasks 50--99 from the selector's posterior on the real ledger. Development rollouts are redrawn for the candidate cells of the real ledger with their counts (\SimTemplate{} of the \ReproRollouts{} eligible pre-freeze rollouts; rows of non-candidates are dropped), with true scores clipped to $[0,1]$. Real episode scores are exactly zero far more often than a Beta distribution allows (\ZeroShareOff\% of reported episodes, \ZeroShareDev\% of these development rollouts), and the veto rule reacts only to exact zeros, so each score is drawn from a zero-inflated Beta: zero with probability $(1-\theta)^{c}$, otherwise Beta with the mean that keeps the overall mean at $\theta$. Least squares on the per-task zero fractions gives $c=\ZeroC$, and moments of the nonzero scores give concentration $\kappa=\Kappa$; the fitted model reproduces the share of zeros. Five rules then pick a route from the simulated rollouts, and we record the true score of what each picked, the score it claimed, and one simulated reporting run of ten episodes per task. Because true scores are drawn from the selector's own posterior, its claims are close to calibrated by construction when the likelihood matches; the informative comparisons are between rules, and under an outcome model that is not Gaussian.

Table~\ref{tab:sim} and Figure~\ref{fig:sim} give four results.
\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Raw means choose worse and overclaim.} Within the model, choosing by raw development mean delivers \SimNaiveTrue{} but claims \SimNaiveClaim{} (optimism \SimNaiveOpt), while the posterior rules claim almost exactly what they deliver (optimism \SimStrictOpt{} for ours). With this many single, zero-heavy rollouts, shrinkage improves both the choice and the claim~\cite{efron2011tweedie}.
\item \textbf{The strict rules cost a little and cut the raw selection bias.} Within the model the rules give up \SimCostOfRules{} against the unconstrained posterior (\SimStrictTrue{} versus \SimPostTrue), but the raw development means of the policies they choose overstate the truth by \SimStrictRaw{} rather than \SimPostRaw. Their value lies outside the model: broken ports, porting errors and outcome distributions the Gaussian misses.
\item \textbf{The candidate pool is the limit.} An oracle choosing among the same candidates reaches \SimOracle, only \SimStrictRegret{} above our rule on these 50 tasks (about 0.02 in \Q). This gap is itself bounded by how strongly the posterior shrinks; the four-task specialist, which delivered 0.58 against an expectation of 0.37, shows the model can understate a strong candidate. Multiplying the development budget by eight raises our rule to \SimStrictEight{} and raw-mean selection to \SimNaiveEight: with plentiful evidence the conservative rules start to cost value.
\item \textbf{Our reported score is unlucky or the expectations were optimistic.} On these tasks the frozen route claimed \ObsClaimed{} and reported \ObsRealized. Among simulated reporting runs of our rule, \ObsRealized{} falls at about the \ObsPct{}th percentile, just below the 90\% range (\ObsLo--\ObsHi). Together with Section~\ref{sec:calibration}, the likelier explanation is that expectations built on mostly single rollouts on one instance were too high.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Rollout noise and statistical power}
\label{sec:noise}
\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\linewidth]{figures/noise.pdf}
\caption{Left: episode scores pile up at zero. Middle: development rollouts concentrated on one instance (none on 320). Right: rollouts per policy needed to detect a difference with a two-sided test at 80\% power ($\alpha=0.05$, unpaired) and to pick the better of two policies with probability 0.8.}
\label{fig:noise}
\end{figure}
Within a task, reported episode scores have a standard deviation of \WithinSDthree{} (\WithinSDnz{} excluding the 29 zero-score tasks); with one rollout per instance this mixes instance difficulty with rollout randomness. \HistZero{} of \Nepisodes{} episodes score below 0.1 (Figure~\ref{fig:noise}). Two framings of power matter. A significance test that two policies differ by 0.05 needs about \PowerFive{} rollouts per policy (\PowerTen{} for 0.10, \PowerTwenty{} for 0.20); this bounds what can be \emph{verified}. Picking the better of two policies with probability 0.8, which is what routing needs, takes about \SelFive{} rollouts per policy for a difference of 0.05, \SelTen{} for 0.10 and \SelTwenty{} for 0.20. Evaluating both policies on the same instances would lower both numbers further. Our eligible ledger falls short even of the selection numbers: of its 404 (task, policy) cells, 198 have a single rollout and 91\% have at most three, and \ShareThirteenElig\% of eligible rollouts (\ShareThirteen\% of all) used instance 313. Before the deadline we estimated that all 54\pl{} remaining per-task switch proposals together were worth about $+0.007$\pl{} in \Q, too little to justify a rerun.

\subsection{Reproducing the selection}
The released selector (\code{policybank.selection}) re-derives the stage-2 decisions for tasks 50--99 from the released ledger, restricted to the \ReproRollouts{} eligible rollouts on those tasks that finished before the freeze. It agrees with the frozen route on \ReproAgree{} of \ReproTasks{} tasks, including all \ReproNondefault{} that route away from the ymetz checkpoint; routing ymetz everywhere would agree on \ReproYmetz. In both disagreements the reimplementation keeps the reference policy. Task 70 is a near tie (posterior 0.176 against 0.173); on task 87 the reimplementation prefers the reference by 0.06 (0.288 against 0.229), where the frozen table had the ymetz checkpoint ahead by 0.035. The original pipeline used a slightly different development table and pooled the two ymetz steps. The 13 stage-2 tasks from 0--49, and all of stage 1, depend on third-party reference tables that we do not redistribute, so they are not reproduced.

\subsection{What did not make the route}
Several ideas were tested on development instances and rejected, or never reached a decisive comparison: replaying a recorded demonstration on development scenes that nearly duplicate demonstration scenes (0 of 14\pl{} such episodes succeeded, mostly from grasp misses), time-compressed action playback, gripper-correction add-ons for the Comet family, sampled action search, a modified robot configuration, 224-pixel camera inputs, and a third-party 100-task model whose port to our serving stack never produced enough nonzero development rollouts to rule out a porting error. Given the power analysis above, ``rejected'' often means ``not shown to help'' rather than ``shown not to help''.

\section{Serving for hidden evaluation}
\label{sec:serving}
Hidden evaluation runs the submitted containers on the organizers' hardware. We package one image per routed policy, and each container is bound at runtime to a single task. The released server enforces a strict contract on every frame and fails closed:
\begin{itemize}
\item one task per container and one evaluator session at a time (a second connection is refused with close code 1013); every new session resets the server's own action queues and the policy wrapper, and an upstream server that keeps per-episode state either receives a forwarded reset frame (opt-in) or must be restarted per session;
\item batches of 1--10 environments with a fixed shape until reset; a reset clears the action queues before the next action;
\item a single global transport chunk $K=4$ that divides both native replay lengths (32 and 20), so the chunked action stream equals the native receding-horizon stream (tested for both lengths and for batches);
\item malformed frames, wrong shapes, nonfinite actions, model exceptions and per-query timeouts (550\,s, below the 600\,s rule) close the connection with code 1011 and stop the server; there is no retry, no substitute action, no fallback checkpoint and no automatic restart, because a fabricated action could turn a fault into a silent wrong result. An evaluator that disconnects is not a policy fault; its session ends and the next one starts fresh. A failure of an upstream policy server, including its connection closing, is a policy fault.
\end{itemize}
Inference runs on a dedicated worker thread, and the command-line entry point leaves the process with \code{os.\_exit} after a failure, so a hung model cannot keep the container alive. \code{OpenPIRemotePolicy} puts the same contract in front of any OpenPI-compatible policy server, which is how the native checkpoints plug in. The release tests all of this against scripted and fake upstream policies. The release also includes a non-root container image for the server and a smoke test that exercises the full contract against it in continuous integration. In the original packaging, all ten native models passed\pl{} startup, warm-up and synthetic-input batch-1 and batch-10 protocol tests on an RTX~3090 as an unprivileged user; the longest batch-10 query took 3.38\pl\,s and sampled GPU memory stayed below 9\pl\,GB. A separate strict-isolation test, which restricted file writes and outbound connections, failed for all ten because CUDA reported no visible GPU\pl; we have not established whether the test harness or the images cause this. Full simulator episodes under Isaac Sim 5.1 at the required one frame per second on the organizers' GPU class have not been run, so hidden-evaluation readiness remains unverified.

\section{Limitations and disclosures}
\begin{itemize}
\item \Q{} is a self-evaluated score on public reporting instances produced with Isaac Sim 6.0.1, not a hidden-test score or a prediction of placement. Hidden evaluation uses Isaac Sim 5.1, and simulator differences may shift scores. The bootstrap interval covers instance and rollout variation only.
\item Development evidence is thin and concentrated on a few instances. Routes for individual tasks are uncertain even where the aggregate ranking is informative, and the calibration analysis suggests that expectations built on one or no own rollouts were too high under both prior types.
\item The selector's Gaussian likelihood is an approximation for bounded, zero-heavy scores; the simulation checks it only against one alternative (zero-inflated Beta) outcome model, and its truths come from the selector's own posterior.
\item Two duplicate cells were resolved by completion order, and seven episodes used a different replay length. Both are disclosed above and left unchanged.
\item We do not redistribute model weights. The publishers of the winner checkpoints and the four-task specialist declare Apache-2.0 at the pinned revisions. The ymetz card defers to its base checkpoint's terms. We did not find an explicit licence for the separately hosted Comet weights or for the specialist used on tasks 96--97. Credits are attribution, not a licence grant.
\item Values marked \pl{} come from pipeline logs and cannot be re-derived from the release.
\end{itemize}

\section{Conclusion}
A bank of public checkpoints, routed per task from development data alone, reached $\Q=\Qval$ on the public reporting instances of the 2026 BEHAVIOR Challenge. Most of the score is partial credit, and 29 tasks scored zero with their routed checkpoint. The more transferable lessons concern measurement. At this scale per-rollout noise is large, a significance test cannot verify per-task gains below about 0.25 with ten rollouts per arm, and estimates built on one rollout are optimistic even after shrinkage. A frozen route and score-blind bookkeeping make the reported number honest; priors, conservative switching rules and a simulation of the procedure itself show how far to trust the route behind it. Code, data and the route are available at \url{https://github.com/FungousLand1941/policy-bank}.
