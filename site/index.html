01

Abstract

The 2026 BEHAVIOR Challenge asks one system to complete 100 long-horizon household tasks in simulation. Rather than train a single generalist, we assemble a bank of ten vision-language-action checkpoints, nine public and one continued by us, and route each task to one of them. Routing uses development instances only: an empirical-Bayes model combines public reference scores, policy levels estimated against a reference policy, and our eligible development rollouts (800 in total, 724 available when the main route froze), and conservative switching rules guard each change of policy. The frozen route reaches Q = 0.156 (95% CI 0.143–0.168) on the ten public reporting instances of every task; 71 tasks score above zero and 37 of 1,000 episodes, in 15 tasks, succeed. Development estimates ranked tasks moderately well (Spearman 0.68) but were optimistic by 0.050 on average, and the gap sits in tasks whose estimate rested on a prior or a single rollout. A posterior-predictive simulation of our own procedure finds that, within the model, ranking by raw development means delivers a worse route and claims about 0.12 more than it delivers, while perfect knowledge of the candidates would add only about 0.04 on the new tasks. We release the route, all 1,000 metric files, the development ledger, the selector, the simulator and a fail-stop serving layer.

02

How the route is chosen

Each task is routed to exactly one policy. The choice uses development instances 311–320 only; the ten public reporting instances (301–310) were run once, after the route was frozen.

Prior for tasks 0–49

These tasks have public 2025 reference scores. A policy's prior mean is a transfer factor times its reference score. For Comet pt50 the factor is estimated from our own development rollouts on the other tasks (0.55 ± 0.09†). For the 2025 winner checkpoints it was fixed at 0.81 ± 0.10† before stage 2; an empirical-Bayes estimate gave 0.69 ± 0.11†.

Prior for tasks 50–99

These tasks have no reference. The prior is policy-level: the reference policy's level plus the mean paired difference on shared tasks, shrunk toward a weak N(0.1, 0.1²) hyperprior and widened by a between-task s.d. of 0.1.

Switching rules

Tasks 0–49, routed in stage 2. The incumbent is the entry from the earlier route. A challenger replaces it only with

  • at least 2 of its own development rollouts;
  • the development guard: it beats the incumbent on at least 2 shared development instances;
  • a posterior gain of at least 0.01;
  • no veto.

Tasks 50–99. The incumbent is the weak reference policy s150997.

  • The highest eligible posterior wins if its gain is at least 0.01;
  • no veto;
  • scope: a specialist is a candidate only on tasks where it has its own development rollouts, and only with at least 2 rollouts in total;
  • one checkpoint per training run.

Veto: a challenger is rejected if it scored 0 on every development instance where the incumbent scored above 0.1.

One checkpoint per training run: the two ymetz steps (6000 and 9999) come from one training run, and only one of them is kept, which keeps the number of serving images small. The search tries every step as the keeper, including steps that won nowhere.

Development ledger and stages

The ledger holds 1,146 development rollouts over 36 labels, 800 of them eligible for selection over 28 labels (724 of these finished before the stage-2 freeze); 47% of eligible rollouts (57% of all) used instance 313.

Stage 1 routed 37 tasks early. These tasks had no incumbent; their entries came from an earlier version of the route writer that ranked posteriors without the guard rules†. The other 63 were deferred, and the stage-2 route was frozen at 12:54 UTC on 13 October from the pipeline's development table (860† rollouts; the released ledger has 724 eligible rollouts finished before then). The guard rules (own evidence, development guard) applied to the 13 stage-2 tasks from 0–49.

Run on the released ledger (411 rollouts on tasks 50–99 that finished before the stage-2 freeze), our reimplementation reproduces 48 of the 50 stage-2 decisions on tasks 50–99, including all 8 that route away from the ymetz checkpoint; routing ymetz everywhere would agree on 42. The 13 stage-2 tasks in 0–49 depend on reference tables we do not redistribute. Of the two disagreements, task 70 is a near tie (0.176 vs 0.173); on task 87 the reimplementation prefers the reference by 0.06 (0.288 vs 0.229).

† From pipeline logs not included in the release.

What the numbers say

Where the optimism comes from

The gap between expected and realized q is +1.5 and +4.1 task points where the routed checkpoint had 0 or 1 of its own development rollouts at freeze, and −0.6 where it had 2 or more. The optimism is not tied to one kind of prior: it appears on the stage-1 tasks, whose priors came from transfer factors (+2.70 task points), and on tasks 50–99, whose priors came from policy levels (+2.60). What both share is thin evidence: estimates that rested on a prior or a single rollout carried the gap.

07

Simulation study

We simulate our own procedure to see which selection rules deliver and which overclaim. The results hold within the model: each simulated world draws true task scores from the selector's own posterior on the real ledger, so the selector's self-consistency is close to guaranteed when the likelihood matches. The informative comparisons are between rules, and under a non-Gaussian outcome model: a rollout scores exactly 0 with probability (1−θ)c, c = 4.0, and otherwise follows a Beta distribution whose mean, θ/(1−π₀), keeps the overall mean at θ, with concentration κ = 6.42. This reproduces the share of exact zeros (0.636 in the reported episodes, 0.740 in the development ledger), which the veto rule depends on.

Each world follows a template of 360 development rollouts from the real ledger on tasks 50–99, keeping their cells and counts. Over 300 worlds we apply five selection rules and compare the Q each rule claims with the Q it delivers.

  1. Raw development means deliver less and overclaim. Ranking by raw means delivers 0.182 and claims 0.117 more than that.
  2. The unconstrained posterior delivers the best route (0.196, claimed 0.198). Our strict rules cost about 0.010 against it within the model, but cut the raw selection bias from 0.079 to 0.045. Their value is insurance outside the model: broken ports and porting errors.
  3. The candidate pool limits the score. An oracle that knows every true score reaches 0.229, about 0.04 above our rules. With 8× the development budget, raw-mean selection overtakes the strict rules (0.219 against 0.197).

Our reported score on tasks 50–99 (0.151) falls below the 5th percentile of simulated reporting runs, at about the 4th percentile (90% range 0.153–0.214): either we were unlucky or the model is mildly misspecified.

08

Serving for the hidden test

The organizers run the hidden test (instances 321–340) on their own machines. Our serving layer is fail-stop: it answers each query exactly as the published contract specifies, or it stops.

  • No retries. A failed query is not re-sent.
  • No substitute actions. The server never sends an action the policy did not produce.
  • No fallback checkpoint. Each task is served by its routed checkpoint only.
  • No automatic restart. A stopped server stays stopped.
  • Malformed frames or non-finite actions end the session with close code 1011.
  • A second connection is refused with close code 1013.
  • Sessions start clean. Every new session resets the server's own action queues; an upstream server that keeps per-episode state gets a forwarded reset (opt-in --forward-reset) or must be restarted per session.
  • Upstream crashes are policy failures. A crash of the upstream policy server ends the session as a terminal policy failure (1011), not as the evaluator disconnecting.

Disclosures

  1. Simulator version. Public results were produced with Isaac Sim 6.0.1, because Isaac Sim 5.1 was incompatible with the cluster's driver. The organizers allowed Isaac 6 for public results; hidden evaluation uses 5.1.
  2. What Q = 0.156 is. It is a self-evaluated score on the public instances. It is not a hidden-test score and not a placement. Its 95% CI resamples instances within tasks; it does not cover selection, the simulator-version shift or the hidden-instance draw.
  3. Duplicate cells. Two cells completed twice. In both, the first completed attempt was kept regardless of score: spraying for bugs, instance 307 (kept 0.5, excluded 0.0), and cook brussels sprouts, instance 303 (kept 0.0, excluded 0.0).
  4. Replay settings. Of the 1,000 episodes, 716 ran with replay 32 and 277 with replay 20. The other 7 ran with replay 0 (one model query per step) in a recovery pass.
  5. Scorer denominator. The organizer scorer copy used a 20-instance denominator, which gives 0.078; the published contract uses 10. No file was edited.
  6. Weights and licences. Model weights are not redistributed here. Redistribution terms for the Comet-derived and amyjia checkpoints are unresolved.
  7. Qualification status. Synthetic-input GPU startup tests passed on an RTX 3090 for all ten models. No full end-to-end hidden-protocol qualification (Isaac 5.1, 1 FPS, Turing GPU) has been completed.
  8. Isolation test. A stricter isolation test (restricted file writes and outbound connections) failed for all ten images because CUDA reported no visible GPU; we have not established whether the test harness or the images cause it.
  9. Unreleased logs. Numbers marked † come from pipeline logs not included in the release.
